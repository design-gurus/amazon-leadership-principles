# Customer Obsession

Customer Obsession means that work starts from the customer's problem and is judged by its effect on the customer. A strong answer names the customer, shows how the candidate learned what that customer needed, and shows a decision that changed because of it.

## What the interviewer listens for

- A named customer. For an engineer, the customer can be an internal team that uses a service or an API.
- Direct contact with the customer's experience: support tickets, usage data, or a conversation.
- A decision where the customer's interest won against convenience, schedule, or a technical preference.
- A measured change for the customer after the work shipped.

## Questions

1. Tell me about a time you changed a plan because of customer feedback.
2. Describe a time you went beyond what a customer asked for.
3. Tell me about a time you had to say no to a customer request.
4. Give an example of a time you worked backward from a customer need to a technical design.
5. Tell me about a difficult customer and how you handled the situation.
6. How do you find out what your customers need?

## Weak and strong answers

| Answer | What it contains |
|--------|------------------|
| Weak | A general statement that customers matter, a project described by its technology, and no evidence that anyone asked the customer |
| Strong | A specific customer, the method used to learn the problem, one trade-off decided in the customer's favor, and a number that shows the effect |

## Sample answer

> "I owned the export feature of an analytics product used by about 900 business accounts. We planned to add three new file formats that quarter. Before starting, I read 140 support tickets tagged 'export' and found that 61 percent were about exports timing out on large reports, not about formats. I presented the ticket count to my product manager and proposed moving formats to the next quarter. I then rebuilt the export as a background job that emails a download link. Timeouts on exports went from 8 percent of requests to under 0.5 percent, and export tickets fell from about 35 a month to 6."
